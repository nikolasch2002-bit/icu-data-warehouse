## 06 Creatinine trend per ICU stay

For each ICU stay, the first, peak and last serum creatinine (mg/dL) measured between ICU admission and discharge, how many hours into the stay the peak came, and the change from first to last. Creatinine rises when the kidneys filter less, so a peak well above the first value points to kidney injury during the stay; query 07 turns that into KDIGO stages. Stays without a creatinine result during the stay are left out.

The trend is rising or falling when the last value differs from the first by at least 0.3 mg/dL, the smallest rise KDIGO counts as kidney injury, and stable otherwise. A stay with a single result has no trend. Only serum creatinine (itemid 50912) is used; the few whole-blood values from blood gas analysers are left out.

## 07 Acute kidney injury (AKI) by the KDIGO creatinine criteria

The highest KDIGO AKI stage reached during each ICU stay, and in-hospital mortality per stage. Each serum creatinine result during the stay is compared with the patient's own earlier results: the lowest in the previous 7 days is the baseline for the ratio criteria, the lowest in the previous 48 hours for the absolute rise. Earlier results count wherever they were taken, so a ward lab the day before ICU admission can be the baseline.

Stage 1: at least 1.5 times the 7-day baseline, or a rise of at least 0.3 mg/dL over the 48-hour baseline. Stage 2: at least 2 times. Stage 3: at least 3 times, or a value of 4.0 mg/dL or more that also meets a stage 1 criterion. KDIGO also stages by urine output and by the start of dialysis; neither is in this warehouse, so stages here can only be underestimates.

A stay whose results have no earlier value within 7 days cannot be staged ('No baseline'). This misses patients whose creatinine was already high when they arrived, a known limit of baselines taken from hospital data only.

## 08 Lactate clearance in the first 24 hours of an ICU stay

Lactate builds up in the blood when tissues get too little oxygen, as in shock. A raised lactate that falls quickly is a sign that resuscitation is working, so lactate clearance is a common target in sepsis care. This query takes the ICU stays whose first lactate in the first 24 hours was 2 mmol/L or more, and compares that first value with the last one taken in the same 24 hours:

clearance % = (first - last) / first * 100

Stays are grouped by whether lactate fell by at least 10%, a threshold widely used in sepsis studies, and in-hospital mortality is shown per group. Lactates taken before ICU admission, in the emergency department for example, are not used, so the first value is the first one in the ICU. The groups are small: read the mortality as a direction, not a rate.

[SQL](08_lactate_clearance.sql)

| clearance_group | icu_stays | median_first_lactate | median_last_lactate | normalised_below_2 | died_in_hospital | mortality_pct |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cleared by 10% or more | 18 | 3.5 | 2.1 | 8 | 4 | 22.2 |
| Cleared by less than 10% | 12 | 3.1 | 3.6 | 0 | 4 | 33.3 |
| No repeat lactate | 6 | 2.2 | 2.2 | 0 | 0 | 0.0 |

## 09 Hyperkalemia and the vital signs around it

A blood potassium of 5.5 mmol/L or more (hyperkalemia) can slow the heart and cause dangerous arrhythmias. For each ICU stay with a potassium of 5.5 or more during the stay, this query takes the first such result and compares the vital signs in the 6 hours before it with the 6 hours after: the lowest heart rate, the highest heart rate and the lowest mean arterial pressure (MAP, non-invasive). Comparing the same stays before and after shows what changed, not just how sick these patients were to begin with.

Flags per stay and period: bradycardia (heart rate below 60), tachycardia (above 100) and hypotension (MAP below 65). Serum and whole-blood potassium both count. A raised potassium can be a lab artefact (from a haemolysed sample), and treatment usually starts within the 6 hours, so the 'after' period shows the combined effect of the potassium and its treatment. Many raised results come soon after ICU admission, before any vitals were charted, so fewer stays have a 'before' period.

| period | stays_with_hyperkalemia | stays_with_vitals | median_lowest_hr | median_highest_hr | median_lowest_map | bradycardia | tachycardia | hypotension |

## 10 ICU-free days at day 28

ICU-free days count the days a patient was alive and out of the ICU in the 28 days after their first ICU admission. A patient who dies within the 28 days gets 0, however long they were out of the ICU before. The measure is a common outcome in critical-care trials because it combines survival and length of ICU stay in one number: higher is better.

One row per patient, starting at their first ICU stay in the data. ICU time counts every stay of that patient in the window, including a return to the ICU in a later admission, cut off at day 28. Deaths come from the date of death (dod), which includes deaths after discharge, so a patient who died at home on day 20 also gets 0.

## 11 Vital signs in the 24 hours before death, against matched survivors

How heart rate, blood pressure, breathing and oxygen saturation change in the last 24 hours of patients who died in the ICU, next to the last 24 hours of ICU stays that ended with the patient leaving alive. For each death, the two surviving stays from the same type of care unit with the closest age are the matches. The deaths are lined up on the time of death, the survivors on the time they left the ICU, and both are cut into four 6-hour blocks counting back from that moment. A survivor's last 24 hours are the hours before they were well enough to leave, so the comparison is between dying and recovering patients.

Within each block, each stay's values are averaged first and the median is then taken across stays, so a stay that was charted every 15 minutes does not outweigh one charted every hour. Survivors are matched with replacement, so one survivor can match two deaths; it then counts once. With 12 deaths the comparison shows patterns, not significance.

## 12 Data-quality report

Checks on the source data that the marts either guard against or pass through, with the number of rows each one finds. Orphan rows point to an admission or ICU stay that does not exist; the facts are built with inner joins and dbt tests, so these checks run on the staging views to show what the source holds. Out-of-range values are physiologically impossible vital signs, such as a heart rate of 0 in a living patient; they usually come from a disconnected monitor or a typing error, and they stay in the marts, so queries that use vitals should keep them in mind. Timing finds vital signs charted before the stay's in-time or after its out-time. Duplicate charting is the same measurement recorded twice at the same time.

The plausible ranges are deliberately wide, so a flagged value is almost certainly an error rather than a very sick patient. A count of 0 is a passed check.
